Introduction

The human vision works roughly as follows: light coming from an object is projected on the curvature of the back of the eye, and our brain flips it and accounts for the curvature, making it flat (mostly). This in fact, is equivalent to perspective projection. There are other type of projections (orthogonal for example), which be later discuessed (WIP?).

The pinhole camera model

The pinhole camera model is very simple camera model that consists of a very narrow hole and a hypothetical box to let light in coming from certain directions, and project it onto a wall that is behind the hole. For our vision, the pinhole camera model is close, though not the same. Our eyes have lenses that focus the upcoming light that alter the aperture, the lens can shrink, get bigger etc. However it is still sufficiently accurate to be able to demonstrate this model for our purposes. Notice in the image that the image is flipped. Now think of an imaginary viewport in front of the pinhole. The image also projects onto that phantom viewport, but the image is, in fact, not flipped:

So imagine your eye sitting at the pinhole, we can say you have a viewport; a plane, at \(z=F\), then perspective project everything there, with respect to the pinhole (your eye)! This is the model we will use mathematically, whether it is rasterization or raytracing.

Perspective projection is a type of transformation. A transformation is esentially a change done to a point, with a specific geometrical operation in mind. For example consider the point: $$P = (2, 3)$$ We can translate, or move this point to the left: \(P' = (0, 3)\) And therefore, we transformed it, we have obtained a point from another by doing something to it, in this case translation.
Perspective projection is esentially the same thing. We take a point and transform it so that it is projected on something, in our case the image plane of our eye. This I think is the most introductory phrase to the conventional real-time rendering methods. Points projecting onto a plane. But for the sake of simplicity, let's start with \(\mathbb{R}^2\), 2 dimensions, as the 2D analogue of a plane is a line.
